## Diagnosis and Staging

### When to Suspect Lymphedema

Lymphedema should be suspected when a limb (or other body part) is persistently swollen, particularly when:
- The swelling does not fully resolve with limb elevation overnight (distinguishing from gravity-dependent edema)
- There is a history of cancer treatment involving lymph node removal or radiation
- Family history of similar swelling (primary lymphedema)
- The **Stemmer sign** is positive: inability to pinch a fold of skin at the base of the second toe (feet) or second finger (hands). This is pathognomonic for lymphedema.

### ISL Staging (International Society of Lymphology)

The current international standard for lymphedema staging:

**Stage 0 (Subclinical / Latency):**
- Impaired lymph transport detectable by functional imaging (bioimpedance or ICG/LSG)
- No visible or palpable edema yet
- May persist for months or years before Stage I develops
- Key window for preventive intervention in post-surgical patients

**Stage I (Early):**
- Visible, pitting edema that reduces with limb elevation overnight
- No significant fibrosis in the subcutaneous tissue yet
- Reversible with treatment

**Stage II (Established):**
- Edema that does NOT fully reduce with overnight elevation
- Progressive fibrosis in the subcutaneous tissues
- May still show some pitting in early Stage II; minimal pitting in late Stage II (fibrosis increasing)
- Not spontaneously reversible, but responsive to CDT

**Stage IIa** (Early Stage II): Some pitting, early fibrosis
**Stage IIb** (Late Stage II): Minimal pitting, established fibrosis

**Stage III (Elephantiasis):**
- Non-pitting edema (fibrosis too dense for pitting)
- Advanced trophic skin changes: acanthosis (skin thickening), hyperkeratosis (rough, callus-like skin), papillomatosis (wart-like skin protrusions), deep skin folds
- Irreversible structural changes; management aims at control, not resolution

**Severity modifiers** (applied within each stage):
- Mild: <20% volume increase vs. unaffected limb
- Moderate: 20–40% increase
- Severe: >40% increase

### ICG Staging (Yamamoto/Tokyo System)

Used specifically for surgical planning (LVA). ICG near-infrared lymphography reveals lymphatic flow patterns:

- **Stage I–II (Linear pattern)**: Visualized lymphatic collectors with defined linear flow → **best LVA candidates** (61% average volume reduction)
- **Stage III (Splash pattern)**: Early dermal backflow
- **Stage IV (Stardust pattern)**: Extensive dermal backflow, dilated collectors
- **Stage V (Diffuse pattern)**: No visible functional lymphatics, complete dermal backflow → LVA less effective (~17% average volume reduction)

### Differential Diagnosis

Before diagnosing lymphedema, consider:
- **Chronic venous insufficiency** (typically presents with brown skin staining, varicose veins, ankle pitting — Stemmer sign negative)
- **Lipedema** (fat distribution disorder — symmetric, bilateral, pain on pressure, spares the feet — Stemmer sign negative)
- **Systemic causes**: Heart failure, nephrotic syndrome, hypoalbuminemia, hypothyroidism — typically bilateral and often reversible with systemic treatment
- **Deep vein thrombosis** (acute onset, calf tenderness)

Lymphoscintigraphy or ICG imaging can confirm lymphatic dysfunction when clinical diagnosis is uncertain.

## Complete Decongestive Therapy (CDT): The Gold Standard

CDT is the **international gold standard first-line treatment** for lymphedema, endorsed by ISL, ILF, NLN, and all major lymphedema professional organizations. It has two phases:

### Phase I: Intensive/Reductive

Typically 4–5 sessions per week, each lasting approximately 1 hour, for 2–4 weeks. The four components:

**1. Skin and nail care**
- Daily cleansing and moisturizing
- Treatment of any fungal infections, wounds, or skin lesions
- This is not cosmetic — damaged skin is the primary portal of entry for cellulitis, which permanently worsens the disease

**2. Manual Lymphatic Drainage (MLD)**
- A specialized massage technique with very gentle, rhythmic skin stretching movements
- Works by: (a) stimulating superficial lymphatic vessels to contract; (b) rerouting lymph flow through alternate, functional pathways (particularly through watersheds connecting different lymphatic territories)
- Performed by a certified lymphedema therapist (CLT) — requires specific training
- Pressure is very gentle (~30 mmHg) — harder massage can damage superficial lymphatics
- Sequence matters: the therapist begins at the neck/trunk to clear proximal lymph vessels before working distally

**3. Multi-layer compression bandaging (MLB)**
- Applied after each MLD session to maintain the volume reduction achieved during drainage
- Multiple layers of short-stretch low-elasticity bandages over cotton foam padding
- Creates a semi-rigid sleeve that: (a) prevents fluid from re-accumulating; (b) enhances the muscle pump effect during exercise
- Worn for approximately 23 hours/day between MLD sessions
- Not comfortable but essential to Phase I outcome

**4. Therapeutic exercise**
- Performed while wearing compression bandages
- Activates the muscle pump, enhancing lymph propulsion
- Specific exercises tailored to the affected limb (arm, leg)

### Phase II: Maintenance (Lifelong)

After intensive Phase I achieves maximal volume reduction:

- **Transition to compression garments** (custom-fitted, flat-knit)
- **Self-MLD**: Simplified drainage techniques taught to the patient for daily home use
- **Self-bandaging**: For patients who need multi-layer compression at night
- **Continued exercise** under compression
- **Vigilant skin care**
- **Regular follow-up** with lymphedema therapist (typically every 3–6 months)

### Evidence for CDT

CDT is supported by multiple randomized trials and extensive observational data. Phase I typically achieves 30–70% volume reduction in Stage I–IIa disease. The challenge is maintenance — without continuous compliance with Phase II, edema gradually returns. CDT manages lymphedema; it does not cure it.

## Compression Therapy: Garments and Bandaging

### Garment Types

**Flat-knit garments** (custom-made):
- Preferred for lymphedema
- Inelastic compared to circular-knit; maintains more uniform pressure and does not "give" as much with sustained force
- Custom-fitted to the individual limb's contours
- Essential for irregular limb shapes (post-surgery changes, fibrotic irregularities)
- More expensive; typically replaced every 4–6 months due to compression decay

**Circular-knit garments** (ready-to-wear):
- Suitable for mild-moderate lymphedema with regular limb shape
- Less expensive; off-the-shelf sizing
- More elastic; may roll or bunch on irregular shapes

### Compression Classes

- Class I (15–21 mmHg): Mild — venous insufficiency, mild edema
- Class II (23–32 mmHg): Moderate — most early lymphedema
- Class III (34–46 mmHg): Firm — moderate-severe lymphedema
- Class IV (>49 mmHg): Extra firm — severe lymphedema; typically requires flat-knit custom garment

Lymphedema typically requires Class II–III.

### Pneumatic Compression Devices (PCDs)

Sequential gradient pneumatic pumps are adjuncts — most useful in the maintenance phase for home use. Multi-chamber devices that sequentially inflate from distal to proximal better mimic natural lymphatic propulsion than simple single-chamber pumps. Evidence supports PCDs as a useful addition to garment therapy for home maintenance.

## Surgical Options

Surgery should be considered when:
- CDT has been optimized but edema is not adequately controlled
- The patient has early lymphedema (optimal timing for function-restoring surgery)
- Prevention is possible at the time of cancer surgery (ILR/LYMPHA)
- Late-stage fibroadipose disease is not responsive to CDT (liposuction indication)

### Lymphaticovenular Anastomosis (LVA)

**What it is:** Supermicrosurgery creating connections between lymphatic collectors (typically 0.3–0.8 mm in diameter) and small adjacent venules. Allows lymph to drain directly into the venous system, bypassing the obstructed lymphatic segment.

**Indications:** ISL Stage I–IIa; ICG Stage I–II (linear pattern). Before significant fibrosis.

**Results:**
- Mean volume reduction: 61% in early disease vs. 17% in advanced disease
- Garment-free rates: 25–40% at 2 years in experienced hands
- Outpatient procedure possible; local anesthesia often sufficient

**Requirements:** Functional lymphatic vessels must still be present (hence the ICG staging). Requires a highly trained supermicrosurgeon (anastomosing vessels < 1 mm diameter under operating microscope).

### Vascularized Lymph Node Transfer (VLNT)

**What it is:** Microsurgical transfer of a pedicle of tissue containing healthy lymph nodes, with their feeding artery and draining vein, to the lymphedematous limb. The transferred nodes may function as biological pumps and/or stimulate new lymphatic vessel growth (neo-lymphangiogenesis) in the recipient area.

**Donor sites:** Inguinal (groin), lateral thoracic (chest wall), submental (chin area), gastroepiploic (omentum), cervical (neck). Each has different complication profiles.

**Indications:** Moderate-advanced lymphedema; Stage II–IIIa; when LVA alone is insufficient or no functional collectors are identified.

**Risk:** Donor-site lymphedema — approximately 5–10% with modern techniques and careful ARM (Axillary Reverse Mapping). The risk of creating lymphedema at the donor site is the major concern with inguinal node transfer.

**Results:** Slower onset of benefit than LVA (results mature over 12–24 months) but often more durable. Volume reductions of 30–50% reported in well-selected patients.

### Combined LVA + VLNT

Growing evidence from case series and prospective studies that combining LVA and VLNT produces synergistic benefits — LVA provides immediate pressure relief while VLNT stimulates long-term biological remodeling.

### Vascularized Lymph Vessel Transfer (VLVT)

An emerging alternative to VLNT that transfers lymphatic vessels with their vascular supply, potentially avoiding the donor-site lymphedema risk of node harvest. Early clinical results are promising.

### Liposuction / Suction-Assisted Protein Lipectomy (SAL)

**What it is:** Surgical liposuction to remove accumulated fatty and fibrous tissue from the lymphedematous limb.

**Indications:** Late-stage (Stage IIb–III) predominantly fibroadipose lymphedema that is non-pitting and not responding to CDT. This stage is dominated by adipose tissue accumulation rather than primarily water.

**Important caveat:** SAL does **not** restore lymphatic function. It reduces limb volume by removing accumulated tissue, but patients must wear compression garments **indefinitely** afterward, or the limb will re-expand. Results are excellent in properly selected patients (often >50% reduction in excess volume) but require lifelong compliance with compression.

**Contraindicated:** In patients who will not reliably wear compression garments lifelong.

### Immediate Lymphatic Reconstruction (ILR / LYMPHA)

**What it is:** At the time of axillary (or pelvic/inguinal) lymph node dissection during cancer surgery, the divided lymphatic vessels are immediately connected to small adjacent veins — preventing lymphatic hypertension before lymphedema develops.

**Evidence:** 13-year meta-analysis (2025) shows ~64–75% risk reduction in BCRL. Best evidence-based prevention strategy available.

**Growing standard:** Increasingly offered at major oncology centers to all patients undergoing level III ALND. Requires a supermicrosurgeon or a surgical oncologist trained in LYMPHA technique to be present at the cancer surgery.

## Infection Management and Prevention

### Acute Cellulitis Treatment

- Oral antibiotics: Penicillin V 500 mg QID or amoxicillin 500 mg TID for 14 days
- Penicillin allergy: clarithromycin or erythromycin
- Severe/systemic: IV antibiotics (penicillin G, cefazolin, or clindamycin)
- Continue CDT and compression during acute cellulitis — this is controversial but supported by current ISL guidelines

### Prophylaxis for Recurrent Cellulitis

For patients with ≥3 episodes per year: **low-dose prophylactic penicillin V** (250 mg BD daily or seasonal in summer months) significantly reduces recurrence. Alternatives include erythromycin or cefalexin. Duration of prophylaxis is typically 1–2 years minimum.

## Special Populations

### Breast Cancer-Related Lymphedema (BCRL)

- The most common cause of lymphedema in high-income countries
- Risk varies with extent of surgery: sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB) ~5–7%, axillary lymph node dissection (ALND) ~29–49%, ALND + radiation up to 70%
- Pre-surgical counseling and baseline limb volume measurement are essential
- ILR/LYMPHA should be discussed with all patients undergoing ALND
- Bioimpedance surveillance post-operatively enables early intervention

### Pediatric and Congenital Lymphedema

- Primary lymphedema requires genetic workup
- CDT is adapted for children — garment design and MLD technique differ
- Children with GATA2 deficiency require urgent hematological referral
- Surgical intervention decisions are deferred in most cases until adolescence unless severe

### Lower Limb Lymphedema (Non-Cancer)

- Often obesity-associated; weight management is paramount
- Tinea pedis treatment essential to prevent recurrent cellulitis
- Flat-knit compression stockings typically Class II–III
- Surgical options (LVA, VLNT) available at specialized centers

### Head and Neck Lymphedema

- Following head-neck cancer surgery and/or radiation
- Internal (pharyngeal, laryngeal) and external components require assessment
- External CDT (face MLD) combined with exercises
- Distinct from limb lymphedema; managed by specialized teams

## Key Guidelines

### ISL Consensus Document (2020/2021)

The **International Society of Lymphology (ISL)** publishes the most widely referenced international consensus guidelines. The 2020/2021 document covers:
- Staging (the 0–III system described above)
- CDT protocol recommendations
- Compression garment prescribing
- Criteria for surgical referral

Available at: isl.arizona.edu

### ILF Best Practice Documents

The **International Lymphoedema Framework (ILF)** publishes disease-specific best practice guidelines and has national frameworks in multiple countries. UK, Dutch, Australian, and North American frameworks provide locally relevant guidance.

### National Guidelines (Netherlands)

For Dutch patients: the **CBO/NHG lymphedema guideline** (NHG = Dutch General Practice guidelines) and the Dutch Society of Dermatology/Plastic Surgery lymphedema protocols. Lymphedema care in the Netherlands is coordinated through lymphedema specialized physiotherapists (lymphtherapeuten) certified by the Nederlandse Vereniging voor Fysiotherapie bij Lymfologische en Oncologische Aandoeningen (NVFL).

Key Dutch reference centers include:
- **Maastricht UMC+**: Major lymphedema surgery program (LVA and VLNT)
- **Erasmus MC, Rotterdam**: Oncological and microsurgical expertise
- **AMC/Amsterdam UMC**: Lymphatic biology research and clinical care
- **LUMC Leiden, Radboud UMC Nijmegen**: Genetic and clinical lymphedema programs

## Summary for Patients

**If you have lymphedema:**
1. **Find a certified lymphedema therapist (CLT)** — CDT is the foundation of treatment
2. **Wear your compression garments consistently** — this is non-negotiable for long-term control
3. **Skin care every day** — daily moisturizing and fungal treatment prevent the most dangerous complication (cellulitis)
4. **Manage your weight** — the single most impactful lifestyle factor
5. **Exercise under compression** — safe, effective, important

**If you are considering surgery:**
- Discuss ICG lymphography staging with a lymphedema surgeon — the pattern determines whether LVA or VLNT is most appropriate for you
- Stage I–IIa patients with linear ICG patterns have the best surgical outcomes from LVA
- Stage III patients with fibroadipose-dominant disease may benefit most from liposuction (SAL)

**If you are facing breast cancer surgery:**
- Ask your surgical oncologist about ILR/LYMPHA — it can reduce your risk of developing lymphedema by 64–75%
- Request a baseline limb volume measurement before surgery
- Ask about bioimpedance surveillance postoperatively for early detection
